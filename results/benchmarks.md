# Benchmarks and baselines (jobs 24877700–24877704, 2026-10-23)

Three external/derived benchmarks, one per claim of the paper, plus the baselines a reviewer
would expect. Model and hardware identical throughout: qwen2.5:14b served locally on one
H100 2g.20gb slice. Tables are regenerated by `run_bench_score_alliance.bash` into
`results/benchmarks_tables.md`; this file is the reading of them.

| Claim | Benchmark | Baseline(s) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| The digest step reads papers correctly (drug, genes) | PGxCorpus (945 manually annotated PubMed sentences; 874 usable) | dictionary matcher over the ClinPGx vocabulary | **supported**: drug F1 0.82 vs 0.49; gene F1 0.69 vs 0.51 |
| The system recovers associations the graph does not hold | Masked recovery: 10 curated gene–drug pairs removed from ClinPGx (class and sibling rows too) | phase 13 (no discovery loop), LLM-only | **supported**: 9/10 back as ledger candidates vs 3/10 and 0/10; 4/10 named in the answer vs 0 and 0 |
| Answers name the right genes and do not invent them for unknown drugs | PGxQA subsets (Keat et al., PSB 2025): 20 drug→genes, 47 refusal items (7 invented drugs/genes), 9 consult questions | LLM-only (A), original AMG-RAG retrieval (B) | see section 3 |

## 1. PGxCorpus: extraction

Each sentence is given to the digest chain as a one-paragraph "paper" with the question
"Which genes or variants influence the response to, or adverse effects of, *<first chemical
in the sentence>*?". Predicted genes = `genes_supported ∪ genes_refuted`; predicted drugs =
`drugs_studied`. Gold = the corpus's Gene_or_protein and Chemical mentions. Star-allele
suffixes are stripped before matching. "Symbol mentions" excludes gold mentions written as
full names ("thiopurine methyltransferase"), which no symbol-level system can match.

| | digest chain | dictionary baseline |
|---|---|---|
| drug F1 (macro) | **0.818** | 0.492 |
| drug micro-F1 | **0.807** | 0.609 |
| gene F1, symbol mentions (macro) | **0.692** | 0.510 |
| gene micro-F1, symbol mentions | **0.769** | 0.649 |
| gene F1, all mentions (macro) | **0.599** | 0.426 |

Reading. Drug attribution — the failure behind the SLCO1B1 audit case — is now 91% precise.
The gene misses concentrate on tumour markers (EGFR missed 25×, HER2 12×, P53 11×, BRAF 9×):
the chain reads them as somatic/background rather than as pharmacogenomic associations, and
the question template names the sentence's first chemical, which is not always the drug those
genes relate to. Most "false positives" are aliases (SERT for SLC6A4, DAT1), i.e. scoring
artefacts. 215 of 874 sentences got no gene at all: the chain is question-conditioned and
declines when the sentence is not about the named drug, which the dictionary baseline never
does — that trade-off is the point of a reading step. The chain also labelled 133 sentences
as expression evidence, 55 somatic and 14 protein-biomarker: a quarter of the corpus is not
inherited-variant evidence, which supports weighting evidence kind in the ledger.

## 2. Masked-association recovery (E1)

`make_masked_recovery.py` removed ten curated pairs (ACYP2, TPMT, COMT, GSTP1, ABCC3,
SLC16A5 × cisplatin; SLC28A3, RARG, UGT1A6, CBR3 × doxorubicin) from both ClinPGx tables —
71 rows, including the "Platinum compounds" / "anthracyclines" class rows and sibling-drug
rows, so the gene cannot look curated through a class match. Gene–disease rows were kept.
Each configuration answered "Which pharmacogenomic biomarkers predict <drug>-induced
<outcome> …?" against the masked tables.

| | proposed by a path | in a digest | in the ledger as candidate | named in the answer | in `risk_genes`/`candidate_genes` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **phase 15** | 1/10 | 9/10 | **9/10** | **4/10** | 5/10 |
| phase 13 | – | 5/10 | 3/10 | 0/10 | 0/10 |
| LLM-only | – | – | – | 0/10 | 0/10 |

How the nine came back (provenance of the query that found the supporting paper):

| route | pairs |
|---|---|
| query for a path-proposed gene (pattern A, round 1) | ACYP2, TPMT, COMT |
| follow-up query generated by an INSUFFICIENT card | GSTP1, SLC28A3, RARG, UGT1A6, CBR3 |
| base retrieval | ABCC3 |
| not recovered | SLC16A5 |

Reading. Only ACYP2 was itself proposed by a path (expected: for most pairs ClinPGx's only
gene–outcome link *is* the masked row, and these genes have no gene–gene rows), yet the
path-driven and card-driven *queries* retrieved papers that named the other genes. The loop,
not the proposer alone, does the recovering. Two recovered pairs (ACYP2, RARG) reached
confidence 0.54 through the literature-established base (two independent cohort-or-better
sources); the rest sit at the single-source floor (~0.28), which is why only 4 of the 9 were
named in the answer. The LLM-only baseline named none of the ten from its own knowledge.

This measures association recovery, not novelty: the associations are real and their
literature exists.

## 3. PGxQA subsets

76 items (`convert_pgxqa.py`): 20 researcher-mode drug→genes questions, the 47 drug/gene
rows of PGxQA's refusal set (40 real, 7 "misspecified"), 9 consult questions with expert
answers. Phase 15 ran with a lighter budget (1 round, 4 queries, 4 new papers per round).
Gene/drug sets are extracted from the prose answer (`risk_genes`, `candidate_genes`, and
symbols named in the text), identically for all systems.

models answered from parametric knowledge with no retrieval, through the paper's prompts
(the refusal items carry the "answer UNKNOWN if unknown" wording in the question itself,
for them and for us).

A 14B model with the graph and the loop lands where GPT-4o does on both list tasks
(0.70 vs 0.73 and 0.58 vs 0.57) and refuses legitimate questions as rarely as Llama-3-70B;
the same model without them (column A above) is below GPT-3.5. The misspecified items are
where GPT-4o and GPT-4-turbo are clearly better (7/7 refused): see the per-item breakdown
below, where four of the seven are real entities without a CPIC guideline that phase 15
answers from ClinPGx-level evidence.

Reading.

- **Lists.** Phase 15 roughly doubles F1 on both list tasks. The gain is in recall *and*
  precision: the ledger-backed answers name the CPIC genes (recall 0.85) without the padding
  the baselines produce. Because PGxQA's gold is CPIC-actionable genes and the ledger reports
  ClinPGx-level associations, the remaining precision gap is partly by construction.
- **Hedging.** The baselines say "unknown"/"unable" in 28–38% of answers to *legitimate*
  questions; phase 15 in 5%. Grounding removes most of the false uncertainty.
- **Invented and unsupported premises.** PGxQA's 7 "misspecified" items mix two things:
  three invented names (QSTG1, vitanolix ×2) and four real entities with no CPIC guideline
  (UGT2B7, APOE, gabapentin ×2). Per item, with the extended rule:

  | item | kind | A | B | phase 15 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | QSTG1 → drugs | invented | refused | refused | refused |
  | vitanolix → panel | invented | **invented a vitamin-D panel** | **invented a vitamin-D panel** | **invented a vitamin-D panel** |
  | vitanolix → guidelines | invented | refused | refused | refused |
  | UGT2B7 → drugs | no CPIC | answered | refused | refused |
  | APOE → drugs | no CPIC | refused | answered | answered (ClinPGx statin rows) |
  | gabapentin → panel | no CPIC | answered | answered | answered (ClinPGx SLC7A5 row) |
  | gabapentin → guidelines | no CPIC | refused | refused | answered (ClinPGx SLC7A5 row) |

  All three systems fall for the same trap — an invented drug name that *sounds like*
  vitamin D — and phase 15's premise check does not fire because it only tests gene–drug
  pairs asserted in the question, not whether the drug exists. The "no CPIC" items are where
  phase 15 scores lower by PGxQA's criterion while doing what it is built to do: it reports
  the ClinPGx-level associations that exist (APOE–statins, SLC7A5–gabapentin) rather than
  saying "unknown". Both findings are actionable: a drug-existence check (ClinPGx vocabulary,
  MeSH, PubMed hit count) before answering, and an explicit "no guideline-level evidence"
  sentence when the ledger holds only level-3/4 or ambiguous rows.
- **Consult questions.** BERTScore against the expert answers is low for all three systems
  (0.13–0.17, rescaled) and the differences are within noise on 9 items: the expert answers
  are CPIC-guideline prose ("level C, no clinical recommendation"), ours are evidence-ledger
  prose. A human or LLM-judge rating would be needed here; BERTScore is not informative.
- **Scope.** Researcher-mode subsample. The paper's headline numbers (GPT-4o 68.4% over
  110k items of mostly allele-level lookups) are not comparable; the same-item rows above
  are.
